> Physics is the study of nature and its laws. Mathematics is its language. Measurement = number × unit.

## Units, Fundamental & Derived Quantities

1. Physical quantities are measured as a numeric part × standard unit (e.g. 2 hours). The units are decided internationally by CGPM (General Conference on Weights and Measures).
2. Fundamental (base) quantities must be (a) independent of each other and (b) able to express every other quantity. Only seven are needed. All others are derived by multiplying and dividing them.

| Base quantity | SI unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length | metre | m |
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Time | second | s |
| Electric current | ampere | A |
| Thermodynamic temperature | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |
| Luminous intensity | candela | cd |

3. Supplementary units: plane angle → radian (rad), solid angle → steradian (sr). Both are dimensionless.

$$\theta=\frac{\text{arc}}{\text{radius}}=\frac{s}{r}\ (\text{rad}),\qquad \Omega=\frac{\text{area}}{r^2}=\frac{A}{r^2}\ (\text{sr}),\qquad \Omega_{\text{full sphere}}=4\pi$$

4. The choice of base quantities is not unique. One could take speed and time as fundamental; then length becomes derived. The SI system was adopted in 1971.

> "New system of units" questions use exactly this idea: choose $c$, $h$, $G$ (or $c$, $G$, $e$…) as base quantities and express length, mass and time through them. In 2026 this appeared as a match-the-list question (Planck length $\sqrt{hG/c^3}$, Planck time $\sqrt{hG/c^5}$, Planck mass $\sqrt{hc/G}$).

## Definitions of Base Units (SI 2019)

5. A standard unit must have invariability and availability. Since 20 May 2019, every SI base unit is defined by giving a fixed numerical value to a universal constant:

| Unit | Fixed constant |
|---|---|
| second | Cs-133 hyperfine frequency $\Delta\nu_{Cs}=9192631770$ Hz |
| metre | speed of light $c=299792458$ m s⁻¹ |
| kilogram | Planck constant $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}$ J s |
| ampere | elementary charge $e=1.602176634\times10^{-19}$ C |
| kelvin | Boltzmann constant $k=1.380649\times10^{-23}$ J K⁻¹ |
| mole | Avogadro constant $N_A=6.02214076\times10^{23}$ mol⁻¹ |
| candela | luminous efficacy $K_{cd}=683$ lm W⁻¹ at 540 THz |

6. SI prefixes: pico $10^{-12}$, nano $10^{-9}$, micro $10^{-6}$, milli $10^{-3}$, centi $10^{-2}$, kilo $10^{3}$, mega $10^{6}$, giga $10^{9}$, tera $10^{12}$.

> Questions such as "a new unit of length equals the distance light travels in 1 s" come straight from this definition. The answer is just distance = $c\times$ time.

## Dimension & Dimensional Formula

7. The dimension of a quantity in a base is the exponent of that base quantity in its expression. Written in square brackets.

$$[\text{force}]=[\text{mass}\times\text{length}\times\text{time}^{-2}]=MLT^{-2}$$

8. Only the type of quantity matters, not magnitude. Change in velocity, average velocity and final velocity all have dimension $LT^{-1}$. Pure numbers ($\tfrac12$, $2\pi$) are dimensionless.

$$[E]=\left[\tfrac12 mv^2\right]=ML^2T^{-2}$$

| Quantity | Formula | Dimensions |
|---|---|---|
| Velocity, acceleration | $x/t,\ v/t$ | $LT^{-1},\ LT^{-2}$ |
| Force, impulse/momentum | $ma,\ mv$ | $MLT^{-2},\ MLT^{-1}$ |
| Work, energy, torque | $Fs,\ rF$ | $ML^2T^{-2}$ |
| Power | $W/t$ | $ML^2T^{-3}$ |
| Pressure, stress, $Y$, $B$, $\eta$ (rigidity), energy density | $F/A$ | $ML^{-1}T^{-2}$ |
| Surface tension, spring constant | $F/l$ | $MT^{-2}$ |
| Coefficient of viscosity | $F/(A\,dv/dx)$ | $ML^{-1}T^{-1}$ |
| Gravitational constant $G$ | $Fr^2/m^2$ | $M^{-1}L^3T^{-2}$ |
| Planck's constant $h$, angular momentum | $E/\nu$ | $ML^2T^{-1}$ |
| Moment of inertia | $mr^2$ | $ML^2$ |
| Boltzmann $k$, entropy, heat capacity | $E/T$ | $ML^2T^{-2}K^{-1}$ |
| Gas constant $R$ | $PV/nT$ | $ML^2T^{-2}K^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}$ |
| Stefan's $\sigma$ | $P/(AT^4)$ | $MT^{-3}K^{-4}$ |
| Charge | $It$ | $AT$ (written $IT$) |
| Potential, emf | $W/q$ | $ML^2T^{-3}A^{-1}$ |
| Resistance | $V/I$ | $ML^2T^{-3}A^{-2}$ |
| Capacitance | $q/V$ | $M^{-1}L^{-2}T^4A^2$ |
| Inductance | $\varepsilon/(dI/dt)$ | $ML^2T^{-2}A^{-2}$ |
| Magnetic field $B$ | $F/(qv)$ | $MT^{-2}A^{-1}$ |
| Magnetic flux | $BA$ | $ML^2T^{-2}A^{-1}$ |
| Magnetic intensity $H$, magnetisation | $B/\mu_0$ | $L^{-1}A$ |
| Permittivity $\varepsilon_0$ | $q^2/(4\pi F r^2)$ | $M^{-1}L^{-3}T^4A^2$ |
| Permeability $\mu_0$ | $B\cdot 2\pi r/I$ | $MLT^{-2}A^{-2}$ |

9. Useful dimension shortcuts (no need to expand):

$$[RC]=\left[\frac{L}{R}\right]=\left[\sqrt{LC}\right]=T,\qquad \left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}\right]=\left[\frac{E}{B}\right]=LT^{-1}$$

$$\left[\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2\right]=\left[\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\right]=\text{energy density}=ML^{-1}T^{-2}$$

> Dimension questions with $\varepsilon_0$, $\mu_0$, $E$, $B$ (e.g. "dimension of $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$", "$B^2/\mu_0$", "$H=\varepsilon^pE^qx^rt^s$") are the most common single type in this chapter. Replace each group with a known physical quantity instead of expanding powers.

## Uses of Dimension

10. **A. Principle of homogeneity:** every term separated by $=,+,-$ must have identical dimensions. So a velocity can never be added to a force. A dimensionally wrong equation is surely wrong. A dimensionally correct one may still be wrong, e.g. $x=ut+at^2$ passes the check.

$$x=ut+\tfrac12at^2:\quad [x]=[ut]=[at^2]=L$$

11. **B. Conversion of units:** since $n_1u_1=n_2u_2$, a derived unit converts through the conversion factors of the base units.

$$n_2=n_1\left[\frac{M_1}{M_2}\right]^a\left[\frac{L_1}{L_2}\right]^b\left[\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right]^c$$

Example: 1 Pa = (1 kg)(1 m)⁻¹(1 s)⁻² = (10³ g)(10² cm)⁻¹(1 s)⁻² = 10 CGS units of pressure. So 1 pascal = 10 dyne cm⁻².

12. **C. Deducing relations** (when the dependence is of product type). Simple pendulum: assume $t=k\,l^a m^b g^c$.

$$[T]=L^{a+c}M^bT^{-2c}\ \Rightarrow\ a=\tfrac12,\ b=0,\ c=-\tfrac12\ \Rightarrow\ t=k\sqrt{l/g}$$

13. Limitations of the dimensional method:
    - You must know beforehand which quantities it depends on, and the dependence must be a product of powers. It fails for sums like $ut+\tfrac12at^2$.
    - Dimensionless constants ($k=2\pi$ above) cannot be found.
    - In mechanics there are only 3 equations (M, L, T), so at most 3 unknown exponents can be found.
    - It cannot handle trigonometric, exponential or log functions. Their arguments must be dimensionless.

> "Find $a$, $b$, $c$" type: write the dimension of every base in the given formula and solve the simultaneous equations. In $y=A\sin(\omega t-kx)$ or $P=P_0e^{-\alpha t^2}$, first make the argument dimensionless. That gives one unknown immediately.

## Order of Magnitude & Structure of the World

14. Write a number as $a\times10^b$ with $1\le a<10$. The order of magnitude is $b$ if $a\le\sqrt{10}\approx3.16$, otherwise $b+1$.

$$\text{diameter of sun}=1.39\times10^9\ \text{m}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{order }10^9$$

15. Basic forces (detail in Physical World): gravitational, electromagnetic, nuclear (strong), weak. Matter: molecules → atoms → electrons and nucleus (protons, neutrons) → quarks.

## Significant Digits

16. A reading has all the certain digits plus one doubtful digit (eye estimate within the least count). Together these are the significant digits. Example: 10.46 cm with a mm scale gives 4 significant digits.
17. Rules for counting significant figures:
    - All non-zero digits are significant. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant (1.0023 → 5).
    - If the integer part is zero, leading zeros after the decimal point are not significant (0.0023 → 2).
    - Trailing zeros in a number without a decimal point are ambiguous. Use powers of 10: $6\times10^2$ (1), $6.0\times10^2$ (2), $6.00\times10^2$ (3).
18. × and ÷: the answer has as many significant digits as the factor with the fewest significant digits.

$$\frac{25.2\times1374}{33.3}=1039.78\ldots\to1040\ \text{(3 s.f.)}$$

19. + and −: line up the decimal points, find the first column (from the left) with a doubtful digit, and round everything to that column.

$$24.36+0.0623+256.2=24.4+0.1+256.2=280.7$$

20. Rounding: next digit > 5, round up. < 5, leave. Exactly 5: if the digit before it is odd, round up; if it is even, leave it. Example: 14.750 → 14.8, 14.650 → 14.6.

> Significant-figure questions (sums or means of readings) appear from 2024 onwards. Apply the addition rule (decimal places), not the multiplication rule.

## Errors in Measurement

21. Errors come from faulty equipment and carelessness (systematic, can be removed once the cause is found) and from random causes (can't be removed, but averaging reduces them).
22. For $N$ repeated readings, the best value is the mean and the uncertainty is the standard deviation $\sigma$. The true value lies within $\bar x\pm1.96\sigma$ with 95% chance, and within $\bar x\pm3\sigma$ with more than 99% chance (valid for $N>8$ roughly).

$$\bar x=\frac1N\sum x_i,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}$$

23. JEE syllabus supplement (NCERT, Unit 1): mean absolute error and propagation of errors. Absolute errors add in sums and differences. Relative errors add, weighted by the power, in products and quotients.

$$\Delta\bar a=\frac1N\sum|a_i-\bar a|,\qquad \delta=\frac{\Delta a}{\bar a},\qquad \%=\delta\times100$$

$$Z=A\pm B:\ \Delta Z=\Delta A+\Delta B;\qquad Z=\frac{A^pB^q}{C^r}:\ \frac{\Delta Z}{Z}=p\frac{\Delta A}{A}+q\frac{\Delta B}{B}+r\frac{\Delta C}{C}$$

> Errors never cancel. Even in $A-B$ or $A/B$, add the errors. And remember the power: a 2% error in the diameter gives a 6% error in the volume of a sphere.

> Error in Young's modulus $Y=\dfrac{4FL}{\pi d^2\Delta L}$: $\dfrac{\Delta Y}{Y}=\dfrac{\Delta L}{L}+2\dfrac{\Delta d}{d}+\dfrac{\Delta(\Delta L)}{\Delta L}$ (least counts used as errors). Similar questions use $g=4\pi^2L/T^2$, $\rho=m/V$, and $R=V/I$.

## Experimental Skills: Vernier & Screw Gauge (JEE Unit 20 supplement)

24. **Vernier calipers:** $N$ vernier divisions = $(N-1)$ main-scale divisions.

$$\text{LC}=1\,\text{MSD}-1\,\text{VSD}=\frac{1\,\text{MSD}}{N}$$

$$\text{Reading}=\text{MSR}+n\times\text{LC}-(\text{zero error with sign})$$

25. **Screw gauge:** pitch = distance moved in one full turn.

$$\text{LC}=\frac{\text{pitch}}{\text{number of circular divisions}}$$

$$\text{Reading}=\text{MSR}+\text{CSR}\times\text{LC}-(\text{zero error})$$

26. Zero-error sign: vernier zero to the right of main zero → positive. Screw gauge: circular-scale zero below the reference line → positive. Always subtract the signed zero error.

> Least count / zero-error questions appeared in 4 of the 8 April-2026 shifts you uploaded, and they are the most common 2024 sub-topic. Also asked: "n MSD = (n+1) VSD" type least counts.

**Tricky points**
- Torque and energy have the same dimensions $ML^2T^{-2}$ but are different quantities. Also: impulse & momentum; stress & pressure & energy density; angular impulse & angular momentum.
- Surface tension = surface energy per area ($MT^{-2}$), the same as a spring constant.
- Dimensionally correct ≠ physically correct.

**Mistakes to avoid**
- Mixing up $B$ ($MT^{-2}A^{-1}$) and $H$ ($L^{-1}A$).
- Treating radian/strain/refractive index as having dimensions.

### Errors, significant figures and instruments

**Question patterns asked again and again**
- % error in a derived quantity ($Y$, $g$, $\rho$, $R$, sphere volume) from least counts — e.g. 2026 • 2 Apr • Morning; 2026 • 6 Apr • Evening; 2022 • 27 Jun • Morning

**Tricky points**
- Subtraction doesn't reduce the error: $\Delta(A-B)=\Delta A+\Delta B$.
- "Ignore the error in the load" means drop $\Delta F/F$.
- Leading zeros are never significant. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are.

**Mistakes to avoid**
- Using the pitch as the least count.
- Adding the zero error instead of subtracting it (or getting its sign wrong).
